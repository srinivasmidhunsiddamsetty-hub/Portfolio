FloodLens explores a more spatially contextual approach to coastal resilience planning, bringing NOAA sea-level-rise scenarios, interactive 3D city visualizations, and location-specific geographic information into a unified experience.

Role Product designer, product owner
Type Web App · GIS · Data Visualization
Timeline Sept 2026 - Nov 2026

01 — The problem

The water was mapped.
The city stayed flat.

Problem Statement

Existing sea-level-rise mapping tools provide valuable geographic information, but largely rely on 2D map-based exploration, making it harder to understand mapped exposure in the context of surrounding buildings and streets.

02 — Who it's for

Municipal resilience planners, urban designers and policy analysts.

The intended value

Help coastal resilience planners compare inundation scenarios, interpret geographic exposure, and identify areas that may require further investigation.

03 — Approach: why 3D

Let the city stand up around the water.

A 2D map shows where the water reaches. FloodLens opens on a 3D city, so you see that alongside building heights and the streets around them. A switch still drops to 2D, where buildings become footprints and the water under them stays visible.

04 — Planning the layout

Controls, map, insights.

I planned the screen around what people do: set the sea level on the left, explore the map in the centre, and read the insights on the right. The map takes most of the screen, so the city stays in view.

05 — First iteration

The app worked end to end.

The plan was on screen with real NOAA data for all of Miami Beach, from 0 ft to +5 ft. It was time for people to use it.

06 — The feedback

Some of the main feedback we received from users.

  1. 1

    The colours didn't separate.

    Neighbouring levels were close shades of blue: "Right now they aren't easy to differentiate."

  2. 2

    The controls hid or misled.

    Turning and tilting the map needed trackpad gestures not every laptop has, and "Watch the water rise" read as tides: "Users think the tides would rise and then go back."

  3. 3

    The right side mixed two jobs.

    The details of the building you clicked shared a panel with a city-wide chart that didn't change when you clicked. The panel also boxed the map in.

07 — Final version

The two changes that mattered most.

1 One job per side.

The left holds the sea level and what it covers. The right holds only a floating card with details about the place clicked on, which frees up the map: the more immersive view the reviewers asked for.

2 Buildings that answer without a click.

As the sea rises, each building changes colour once mapped water touches its footprint, so the 3D map reads without a click.

Major fixes from the review

Easier to tell apart, and easier to control.

  1. 1

    Colours that separate.

    Overall shows the water in one blue. Per foot gives each foot its own colour, from orange to violet.

  2. 2

    A slider that reads as rising.

    It stands upright, and the button that suggested tides is gone.

  3. 3

    One toolbar, no gestures.

    Zoom, turn, tilt and "Entire Miami Beach" sit together, with tooltips.

08 — Outcome

From a top-down map to the city in 3D.

09 — Reflection

A feature people can't find might as well not exist.

— Closing reflection
Going in

I built turning and tilting into the map itself, so the 3D view could be explored by hand.

↓
Coming out

It relied on trackpad gestures not every laptop has. Putting zoom, turn, tilt and the whole-city view in one toolbar, with tooltips, made the 3D view usable by anyone. Now I check that every key action has a visible control.
